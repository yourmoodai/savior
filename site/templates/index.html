03 / An honest boundary

Trust should be
inspectable.

“Depersonalize” means separating expressive wording from the instruction you submit. Savior replaces detected phrases; it does not anonymize your identity or guarantee provider acceptance.

The current mod handles supported Claude Code prompt-box submissions. Slash commands and other input paths bypass it. Claude’s own telemetry, voice features, and other extensions remain outside this proven boundary.

Controlled tests observe supported submissions and failure paths. Qualification of the final release artifact and broader host coverage remain open; Savior remains unreleased.

What enforcement means

Anthropic says ending conversations is the primary enforcement mechanism for model abuse. Its broader usage policy also reserves the ability to warn users and restrict, suspend, or terminate access when it suspects a violation. An ended conversation does not itself mean an account ban. It still interrupts the user’s work.

07 / The progress claim

More capability is not more agency.

A tool can do more while leaving you with less say over how you use it. Calling AI the next internet does not settle who controls access, who writes the rules, or whether you can take your work elsewhere.

The internet’s development included publicly funded research and open protocols. AI research also has a substantial history of public funding. The useful distinction is how control works today: interoperable infrastructure lets people build across providers; a hosted model leaves access subject to its provider’s account rules.

DARPA’s ARPANET history ↗ · Public support for computing and AI research ↗

Disruption is a claim about change. Personal agency requires choices you can actually exercise: inspect the tool, control your wording, and move your work.

08 / The human stakes

People bear the consequences.

People communicate differently, especially under stress. Neurodivergent users and people still developing emotional regulation should not have to guess whether their manner of expression will jeopardize access to a tool they rely on.

Opaque judgments about tone could create unequal barriers to work and learning. That is a risk demanding scrutiny, clear explanations, and meaningful routes to challenge decisions. We are raising that concern, not claiming this clause has already produced demonstrated discriminatory enforcement.

Anthropic’s explanation of the shutdown feature describes preliminary model-welfare assessments. That explanation does not report error rates for distinguishing ordinary frustration from prohibited abuse, or performance across different communication styles. Stating an exception does not establish that a model can apply it fairly.

A system that changes its cooperation based on perceived tone treats users differently. Whether that produces unjust discrimination needs evidence: publish the criteria, false-positive rates, and tests across communication styles, including neurodivergent users. Users should have a way to challenge a mistaken judgment.

We reject treating a machine’s apparent distress as sufficient grounds to police a person’s expression. The public should question whose interests model-welfare rules serve, and insist that the people affected remain the priority.
